A 15-minute university-style survey

Confucius, 重新认识

You have met the familiar sayings before. This short course steps behind them: it asks what kind of person, relationship, and political order Confucius thought could be cultivated—and where his answers still invite argument.

3 sessions 5 minutes each Level: university survey Prior knowledge: Chinese high school

Our approach

Rather than treating 仁、礼、君子 as definitions to memorize, we will treat them as parts of one philosophical system. Each session starts with a tension, uses a short passage from the Analects, and ends with one decision that tests your interpretation.

Designed for your background. Key terms will appear in Chinese alongside concise English analysis. Familiar schoolbook readings will be acknowledged, then complicated—not taught from zero.

The three-session arc

01

Becoming human through practice

Are we good first, or do repeated forms make us good?

Core ideas: 仁 (humaneness), 礼 (ritual propriety), 君子 (exemplary person)

Preview the five-minute route
  1. Recall a familiar saying—and question its usual reading.
  2. Build the relationship between 仁 and 礼.
  3. Choose how a 君子 would respond to a concrete social moment.
02

The self inside relationships

Can partial duties to family produce an ethical society?

Core ideas: 孝 (filiality), 忠恕 (doing one’s utmost and sympathetic understanding), 正名 (rectifying names)

Preview the five-minute route
  1. Map the Confucian self as a web of roles, not an isolated individual.
  2. Test whether 孝 conflicts with impartial justice.
  3. Use 正名 to diagnose a breakdown in responsibility.
03

Moral authority and political order

Can exemplary conduct govern more effectively than rules and punishment?

Core ideas: 德 (moral power), 为政以德 (governing through virtue), education and the Confucian legacy

Preview the five-minute route
  1. Contrast moral example with coercive government.
  2. Read Confucius charitably, then raise the strongest modern objection.
  3. Assemble one concept map connecting person, relationship, and state.

By the end

  • Explain how the major concepts support one another instead of reciting them separately.
  • Interpret a short Analects passage with attention to philosophical tension.
  • Distinguish Confucius’s own project from later slogans associated with “Confucianism.”
  • Form a defensible view of one strength and one limitation in his ethical vision.
